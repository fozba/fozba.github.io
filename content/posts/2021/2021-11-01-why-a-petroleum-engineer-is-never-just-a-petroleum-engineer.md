## Why a Petroleum Engineer Is Never Just a Petroleum Engineer

I like complicated introductions.

However, at the end of this post, I hope I will explain what this title means at all.

Petroleum Engineering is, I believe, one of the broadest engineering branches. That is because, in order to be a proficient petroleum engineer, you have to have at least a basic knowledge about many different branches of engineering. More importantly, you have to have a good knowledge of several branches.

Let me start elaborating by asking “What is an engineer?” Wikipedia’s answer to this basic but profound question is: *“Engineers, as practitioners of engineering, are professionals who invent, design, analyze, build and test machines, complex systems, structures, gadgets and materials to fulfill functional objectives and requirements while considering the limitations imposed by practicality, regulation, safety and cost.”* A simpler answer which I prefer is:

*An engineer is an individual who applies science to complete challenges.*

When you start to think in this sense, and not in “Engineer is a person who sits on a desk 9-to-5 and rearranges some Excel sheets”, you start to feel the breadth of the concept. It is damn strong! To emphasize the concept a little bit more, let’s review some engineers and engineering applications around the globe.

I’ll go as far as saying this nut-cracking procedure is indeed engineering. Although primitive, the example fulfills all the requirements:  
**Challenge:** The nut is a nutritious food. However, the shell must be cracked first.  
**Scientific Knowledge:** The rock has higher strength than the nut. If they collide with a force, the nut is likely to be cracked.

Or, according to Wikipedia’s definition: *“<span style="color: red;">Engineers, as practitioners of engineering</span> (Chimpanzee), <span style="color: red;">are professionals who</span><s> invent, design</s>, <span style="color: red;">analyze</span> (assessing the strength of the rock, note that the chimpanzee does not use another material), <s>build and</s> <span style="color: red;">test</span> (testing if the stone can crack the nut) <s>machines, complex systems, structures, gadgets and </s><span style="color: red;">materials</span> (the stone) <span style="color: red;">to fulfill functional objectives and requirements</span> (cracking the nut to eat it) <span style="color: red;">while considering the limitations imposed by practicality</span> (it is efficient to break nuts with stones), <span style="color: red;">regulation</span> (no regulations in jungle), <span style="color: red;">safety</span> (baby chimp standing aside) <span style="color: red;">and cost</span> (stones are abundant).”*¹

Maybe a more familiar example can be Saturn V, the first giant leap of mankind:

Let’s view this wonderful example in the same manner:  
**Challenge:** Land humans on the Moon and retrieve them back to Earth while conducting scientific experiments and observations in a safe manner.  
**Scientific Knowledge:** Cumulative knowledge of humanity in terms of rocket physics, chemistry, thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, fluid dynamics, … you get the idea.

Whether it is a primitive job such as cracking some nuts or defying gravity, engineering is just about solving real-life problems by harnessing the power of scientific knowledge.

Now, what about **petroleum engineering**? If you are in the petroleum or geology industry, you probably know that some petroleum engineers work in geothermal exploration & production, as well as petroleum E&P. That simply means a petroleum engineer can also start a career in geothermal energy. In addition, a petroleum engineer learns quite a lot about geothermal E&P throughout their Bachelor’s program. Thus, in my humble opinion, the title *Petroleum Engineering* is incomplete and should be replaced as *Subsurface Fluid Engineer*. When you start to think in this sense, you will realize that a petroleum engineer must have at least a basic knowledge about:  
• Fluid mechanics  
• Fluid dynamics  
• Geophysics  
• Engineering statics & mechanics  
• Petroleum chemistry  
• Thermodynamics (You’ll need this bad boy for pretty much anything)  
• Calculus with a solid background on differential equations (I think I don’t need to explain why you have to know DE)

Aside from the things above, since you want to be able to invent, design, build systems and structures to solve any kind of subsurface fluid problems, you have to know how to make tools. Nowadays, the best way of making tools is to know programming and using design software, so we can surely add more topics to our bullet list:  
• Programming  
• A bit of numerical methods, if you want to stay sharp about implementing your code  
• One CAD software  
• A bit of algorithms and data structures, if you want to go deeper about making tools

When you add all the stuff in the curriculum to the list, such as:  
• Drilling engineering  
• Reservoir engineering  
• Production engineering  
you realize that you have a pretty big list. Of course, no one can be a master of all those at once easily—some of them (like Fluid Dynamics) have their own academics who dedicate their entire academic career to that specific topic. However, if you are going to practice engineering, you have to know at least a thing or two about all these to *be able to* apply the knowledge. You can’t read a Korean IKEA Table Manual if you don’t know Korean at all, no matter how good you are with making tables. That is why you need to know these at least to keep your learning curve efficient.

That is a lot of talking, but I think I explained why a Petroleum Engineer is never only a Petroleum Engineer, with the help of chimpanzees and rocketships. That is why I advise all like-minded people to think of themselves as “Subsurface Fluid Engineers” and to not just limit themselves with hydrocarbons.

That is all folks!
