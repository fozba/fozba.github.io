*Before we start: [What is group mind?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_mind_(science_fiction))*  
This is going to be a long one (and a *highly* fictional one), grab your cup of coffee!

There have been numerous inventions and discoveries throughout the entire history of humanity. Some of them introduced only practical ease to our daily life, and their impact wasn’t that great, like, for example, the back scratcher or shoelace. Some of them introduced practical ease but made a much greater impact, for example, the automobile and airplane. Some of them, however, are more exclusive by having a massive impact on our lives. So what are those, and more importantly, is there a way to actually “categorize” those discoveries and inventions, or is there a way to separate the most important ones from the others?

I know the importance of these is very subjective, and there’s most probably not a universal way of sorting and/or categorizing the inventions. It is *logically* impossible (here I refer to mathematical logic and not daily life logic, so you can read it as “scientifically” if you want) to prove that a back scratcher is less important than Saturn V, but we can follow some kind of consensus here. And why we’re going to do this is hidden in the title. Needless to say, before delving deeper, these are all byproducts of my mind, which is naturally biased by my mind’s interests.

In order to obtain a pattern, let me dig the internet for the most important discoveries and inventions. There are tons of online sources pointing out different answers, as expected, since the topic is highly subjective. However, again, we can follow the consensus and mark out the most mentioned ones, which are roughly: **Printing press**, **Light Bulb**, **Electricity**, **Computer**, **Vaccination**, and **Internet**. Even though not all of them fit a category, I think there’s a very insightful quality of some of those greatest inventions. Consider the printing press—the one considered the most important by nearly all of the sources—electricity, and the internet. What do these have in common? One thing: Increasing the *data transmission* rate (or efficiency) of a species, that is, being able to transfer and receive more information with less effort.

Human beings are cooperative creatures, and they utilized this characteristic to prevail over the harsh conditions of nature. Cooperation is everything for us. But this cooperation isn’t limited to basic work sharing, or it’s not just “hunting with a pack.” It’s more than that. It’s the ability of *sharing information*. The ability of telling someone to avoid a place full of wild animals was essential for our ancestors. They did that. They *taught* each other about foraging, hunting, survival, and all sorts of stuff.

The punch-line here is that most of the great inventions we see are about enhancing this information-sharing process. Think of the very first times: When you see a lion, you scream. When you see the Sun, you are amazed. But you can’t express all these emotions with random shouting, which is why **you invent the language** (although language is considered a discovery by some, in my opinion, today we use language to define and transfer abstract information about abstract objects, such as mathematical entities, which makes the syntax and organization way too “complex” for it to be considered a discovery). Now, when you point at a hill and shout “Lion,” your best pal Mr. Caveman knows there’s a lion on the hill, and it’s best to avoid that hill when gathering food (didn’t pay serious attention to the archaeological correctness of the anecdote, remember, I’m an engineer, lol). But what’s next? Okay, you know where the lions are, where the good foods are, and all. However, now you want to be able to *store* this information so that it’s accessible to anyone. Sure, old tales and legends work great, but the quality of the information isn’t retained that way, and it’s very disappointing to see that the lion you told about to your friend Mr. Caveman became a 3-headed, snake-tailed, fire-breathing dragon that needs to be worshipped every once in a while. So what do you do? **You invent writing**. Now you can carve some symbols on those stone tablets, describing how awesome you were and why you should not be forgotten at all. With good craftsmanship, you can use a small font and shrink 2kB worth of text on a heavy-ass tablet. But this isn’t enough. You’re human. You want to do more. You want not only to save storage space (literally) but also want the storage to be *mobile*. That’s why, next, **you invent the paper**. Now you can store the same information in a much smaller and lighter object.

Up to this point, the bottleneck was storage. But that’s no more. Now the bottleneck is the *quantity* of the information. Meanwhile, you invented a lot of cool stuff, and you want to be able to share this information with as many members of your species as possible. You write a 100-paged book. You realize this is so frustrating. You pay some people to write copies of your book. They realize it’s so frustrating, but they keep doing it because it earns them money. But it’s a slow process, and the books are consequently unreachable by the masses because they’re so expensive. Remember, you paid a lot to one maniac to repeatedly write your 100-paged book for 100 times, every time the same, no punctuation mistakes, no grammar mistakes. But then, you come up with a bright idea: A process for writing books automatically. And that, ladies and gentlemen, is the **printing press**, the most important invention of all time according to the internet.

Up to this point, the bottleneck was quantity. But that’s no more. Now the bottleneck is the *speed* of the information. Sure, there’s enough information for everyone. Now there are newspapers and everything. That’s why, now, what determines the efficiency of information transfer is the time it takes for the newspaper to be delivered to the reader. Although an ordinary person (for example, you or me) would be pretty much satisfied with the status quo, guys who love thinking and experimenting aren’t. They don’t want to wait for a letter about a scientific question. It’s a slow process. That’s why they invent the **radio**, **telephone**, and **television**. Now the information is spreading like crazy, but still not enough. Some people want to learn what happened on the other side of the world in seconds; some others want to store a whole library somewhere that is *accessible by everyone*. This is all crazy, and huge respect to those who invented all these tools! Turning back to the topic, then there comes—my favorite—the **internet**. Now everyone can see my dinner plate with heavenly-vivid colors and read the stupid quote I wrote underneath.

So, what’s next? How do we even enhance information further? The rest is totally speculation, and I’ll brainstorm & present my ideas:

When I look at the current status of information delivery, I can see only one bottleneck: *Information creation* speed. This doesn’t mean creating something new, but rather it means I have to “type” these words in this blog to “create” the information. And typing isn’t that fast. The same goes for talking. **All of those are slower than our thoughts**. That’s why, probably, the next great invention will be a human-computer interface that will allow us to interact with the computer by *thinking*. I’m heavily rooting for Neuralink for this one, but fate is a mystery after all. This “human-computer interfacey machine thing” will be the next giant leap, but there’s simply way too much to talk about it, so I’m saving it for the second post of the series.

After this hypothetical “human-computer interface” mentioned above has advanced significantly, I believe we will unleash the secrets of the human brain and have a way better understanding and control over how it works. This process may lead to crazy stuff like mind uploading, etc., but the punch-line here is that at some point, we will be able to imitate the human brain in a digital environment (although it’s pretty much debatable whether we can simulate a brain within a computer, since the human brain isn’t Turing-complete), which will usher in a new era in consciousness (sorry if I’m going very bold here, lol). After that, there will be only *one* next thing (at least for *Homo sapiens*, not for humans though), which is about removing the last bottleneck. Drum roll here!!! Are you ready?

Individuality.

I think evolution forces all intelligent species to form some kind of hive-mind eventually. Some do this early, and some do this late. Some never reach that point, but I think the actual “game” starts after singularity—if, of course, the hive-mind of humanity, stripped away from all its previous emotions, wouldn’t decide that the effort of survival is unnecessary, and we can simply cease to exist (this could be the answer to the Fermi paradox, but I don’t want to get that wild for now).

Don’t get me wrong. I love individuality. I never think it’s something bad. After all, it’s *me*, right? However, I sort of believe that individuality is actually going to be the strongest bottleneck in all our *communication*, provided that the crazy things I’ve listed above have happened. That’s because we’re very far away from defining a logical consensus that will be valid not only for mathematical and scientific applications but also for every situation. We’re still very inefficient at processing a lot of information. And some of this information is very, very valuable. Think of philosophical information. It’s one of the most valuable types of information, if not the most valuable one. But it’s very hard to establish a logical consensus and progress in such important fields. Maybe a post-human organism that consists of the minds of many humans will establish that much-needed consensus somehow, and that will sky-rocket our information-processing capabilities. Imagine that you’re one person and you’re the best physicist, best mathematician, best philosopher, best engineer, and everything. You have the information that all of humanity possesses. It would be something otherworldly.

Although I believe technological development will lead us to some kind of group-mind, even with my super speculation powers, I can’t argue that it’s the ultimate evolution. I only think that at some point, this will be *encountered*. What has been created might be something that no one wants to “join,” or it could simply destroy itself. The worst thing about singularity is that it’s total chaos, and it’s very much unpredictable with today’s knowledge. Who knows, maybe we accidentally create *Master* from the *Fallout* series?

That’s why, whether we’ll join the Stellaris session of this universe as a Gestalt consciousness or we simply invoke the 3rd World War and start polishing our clubs for the 4th one, the discussions about normative sciences are now of the utmost importance, since we need to guarantee that we’re culturally ready for the inventions of the new era. After all, nuclear fusion is a great discovery, but a species is only *intelligent* when they prefer using it to light cities instead of bombing them.
